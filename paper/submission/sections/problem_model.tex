\section{The process selection and lot-sizing problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Industrial setting}
\label{sec:setting}

We study the grain-finishing stage of a manufacturer of electrofused
grains (silicon carbide and fused aluminum oxide). Upstream, raw
materials are fused in electric furnaces and cooled into blocks of
roughly twenty tonnes; the finishing stage crushes, mills, and screens
this material into final products defined by granulometric ranges and
physical--chemical specifications. The finishing equipment can be
arranged in a number of alternative configurations -- the
\emph{production processes}. Two features of these processes drive the
planning problem. First, \emph{co-production in fixed proportions}:
operating process $j$ for one period yields a technologically
determined quantity $a_{ij}$ of every product $i$ simultaneously; the
planner selects processes, not product quantities. Second,
\emph{setup magnitude}: reconfiguring and cleaning the equipment
between processes consumes a substantial fraction of a planning
period, which motivates a big-bucket representation in which at most
one process is operated per period. Demand stems from an order book
with due dates; the commercial priority is meeting the order book, so
the objective minimizes total shortage, with a small penalty
discouraging gratuitous excess production (excess is held as
inventory and may serve future orders, but ties up capital and
storage). \todo{One paragraph with anonymized company facts: number

\subsection{Mathematical model}
\label{sec:model}

Let $I$ denote the set of products, $J$ the set of processes, and
$T=\{1,\dots,|T|\}$ the set of periods. Parameter $a_{ij}\ge 0$ gives
the output of product $i$ when process $j$ is operated for one
period, and $d_{it}\ge 0$ the demand of product $i$ due at period
$t$. The decision variables are $x_{jt}\in\{0,1\}$, equal to 1 if
process $j$ is operated in period $t$, and the nonnegative continuous
variables $f_{it}$ and $e_{it}$, measuring the shortage and the
excess, respectively, of product $i$ with regard to its
\emph{cumulative} demand up to period $t$. The model, referred to as
MFP (minimization of shortage of production, after
\citealp{luche2009combining}), reads:
%
\begin{align}
\min \quad & \sum_{i\in I}\sum_{t\in T}\bigl(f_{it} + \gamma\, e_{it}\bigr)
  \label{eq:obj}\\
\text{s.t.} \quad
& \sum_{j\in J}\sum_{\tau\le t} a_{ij}\, x_{j\tau} + f_{it} - e_{it}
  \;=\; \sum_{\tau\le t} d_{i\tau},
  && i\in I,\; t\in T, \label{eq:dem}\\
& \sum_{j\in J} x_{jt} \;\le\; 1, && t\in T, \label{eq:prop}\\
& x_{jt}\in\{0,1\},\quad f_{it},\,e_{it}\ge 0, && i\in I,\; j\in J,\;
  t\in T. \label{eq:dom}
\end{align}
%
Constraints~\eqref{eq:dem} balance cumulative production against
cumulative demand for every product and period, so that $f_{it}$
captures lateness with respect to due dates (not only end-of-horizon
shortage), and constraints~\eqref{eq:prop} allow at most one process
-- possibly none -- per period. The weight $\gamma=0.001$ makes
excess lexicographically subordinate to shortage in practice. This
term is a deliberate refinement of the original formulation
\citep{luche2009combining}, which minimized shortage alone under an
inequality balance: because $e_{it}$ measures \emph{cumulative}
excess, every unit produced ahead of demand is charged in each
subsequent period, so $\gamma$ acts as a proxy for the capital and
storage tied up by early production. The refinement is not
computationally innocuous, and Section~\ref{sec:gamma} quantifies its
cost. The
model has $|J|\,|T|$ binary and $2|I|\,|T|$ continuous variables; for
the largest instances considered here ($|J|=159$, $|T|=190$) this
amounts to 30{,}210 binaries. Table~\ref{tab:instances} summarizes
the dimensions of the full benchmark family.

Model MFP is a single-stage, big-bucket member of the simultaneous
lot-sizing and scheduling family \citep{copil2017simultaneous}, with
an assignment-type setup structure per period; its distinctive
element is that each period's assignment releases the fixed output
vector $(a_{ij})_{i\in I}$, coupling all products through
constraints~\eqref{eq:dem}.

\subsection{Instances and empirical difficulty}
\label{sec:difficulty}

Our benchmark derives from a single real order book of the plant
($|J|=159$ processes, 50 items, 19 daily periods). From it, the
original study generated ten instances by redistributing each item's
total demand at random over the horizon, preserving aggregate volumes;
we inherit this set (denoted Real, $|T|\in\{19,25\}$, one instance
using a longer reserved horizon) and the horizon-replicated
sets obtained by the rule documented in the original study
\citep[Section~6.2.2]{daleluche2011tese}: the number of periods is multiplied by
$k$ and the demand of the original horizon is repeated exactly in
each 19-period block, yielding sets 2X--5X ($k\in\{2,\dots,5\}$,
inherited) and the new sets 8X and 10X introduced in this paper
($k\in\{8,10\}$, $|T|$ up to 190). Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}
details the generation and its validation against the inherited
sets. Demand is therefore periodic by construction across the whole
family -- a property we state explicitly because it keeps the
generation rule constant along the scale axis, avoiding confounds;
empirically, periodicity does not make the instances easy for the
solver (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

Two empirical observations shape the experimental design. First,
difficulty is heterogeneous within sets: the subclass
%
\begin{quote}\footnotesize
3X: IncT3x\_1, IncT3x\_3, IncT3x\_7, IncT3x\_9, IncT3x\_10;\\
4X: IncT4x\_1, IncT4x\_3, IncT4x\_7, IncT4x\_9, IncT4x\_10;\\
5X: IncT5x\_1, IncT5x\_3, IncT5x\_7, IncT5x\_9, IncT5x\_10
\end{quote}
%
exhibits both above-median solver gaps and above-median BKS-to-bound
gaps within its set -- on
IncT5x\_10, the best known solution still lies about 17\% above the
three-hour dual bound, so the true optimality gap is unknown.
Second, the solver's parallel search, although deterministic for a
fixed budget, is budget-nonmonotone in the primal sense: on three
instances the one-hour run returned a better incumbent than the
independent three-hour run. Both observations motivate reporting
gaps against best-known solutions (BKS), defined as the minimum
objective over \emph{all} methods, budgets, and seeds considered in
this study, rather than against dual bounds alone.
