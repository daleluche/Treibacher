\section{Computational experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Benchmark and public release}
\label{sec:benchmark}

The benchmark comprises the plant's real order book and 60 derived
instances in six sets, all sharing its technology matrix
($|J|=159$, $|I|=50$). Set S holds the ten instances obtained by
random redistribution of item demand
(Section~\ref{sec:difficulty}), sets 2X--5X the inherited horizon
replications, and sets 8X and 10X the new ones introduced here, with
five instances each. We reimplemented the generator and validated it
by regenerating every inherited instance from the corresponding member
of S: all 40 files of sets 2X--5X match record by record in horizon,
products, technology matrix and demand. The generator is
deterministic, and the manifest records execution provenance only.
Because the derived instances redistribute demand rather than
reproduce it, they carry no commercial schedule of the company, and
the real order book itself is released with its dimensions and
volumes but without any identification of the firm. All instances, solutions,
per-run trajectories, and per-window logs are released at
\todo{Zenodo DOI}.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

All experiments ran on a single machine (AMD Ryzen~7 5800H, 8~cores /
16~threads, 32~GB RAM) with CPLEX~22.1.2 called through GAMSPy,
deterministic parallel mode, and threads set explicitly to all cores
for every method, including window subproblems. Methods are compared
under equal wall-clock budgets of 600, 3{,}600, and 10{,}800~s, with all model-generation and bookkeeping overhead charged to the
budget. The \ILS{} baseline runs ten parallel workers per instance
and reports the best of ten, a deliberately generous allocation
(Section~\ref{sec:metaheuristic}). Best-known solutions (BKS) are
the minimum objective over all methods, budgets, and seeds, subject
to a consistency safeguard that rejects any candidate below an
available dual bound; this safeguard exposed evaluator
inconsistencies in the legacy heuristic of the original study, whose
results we therefore exclude from comparison tables (they remain in
the released raw data, flagged). The central comparisons were stated
as hypotheses H1--H4, with explicit decision rules, in the campaign's
planning documents before the corresponding experiments were run, and
a cross-budget question Q5 was stated before the final solver runs.
These are working documents rather than an entry in a public
registry, so we make no claim of formal pre-registration; we do report
every outcome against the criterion as originally written, including
the two hypotheses that were not supported, and we label separately
every analysis carried out after seeing the results. Matheuristic
parameters were tuned on the two hardest 5X instances
(Section~\ref{sec:fo}); both belong to the evaluation set, a choice
we disclose and weigh when interpreting results on those two
instances. Unless stated otherwise, all results refer to the model
with $\gamma=0.001$; the $\gamma=0$ variant appears only in
Section~\ref{sec:gamma}.

\subsection{The problem's practical status today}
\label{sec:progress}

The original study concluded that instances of practical size defeated
the MIP technology available at the time, which is what motivated
fifteen years of heuristic development for this problem
\citep{luche2009combining}. That conclusion no longer holds at the
scale of the plant. With CPLEX~22 on a current laptop, every instance
of the Real set is solved to proven optimality in seconds (mean wall
time 8~s), and every instance of 2X within the one-hour budget (mean
687~s, all proven optimal). Difficulty reappears only from 3X upward:
at three hours, mean gaps grow from 1.7\% (3X) to 3.9\% (4X) and 7.2\%
(5X), with no proven optima in those three sets.

The real order book itself provides an independent check on the
whole apparatus. Its optimal shortage was reported as 10{,}475~kg in
the original study \citep[Section~4.2]{luche2009combining}, obtained
in 26~s by the solver of the time. Our reconstruction of that instance,
solved here under the model of Section~\ref{sec:model}, returns
exactly the same shortage, 10{,}475~kg, at an objective of
11{,}442.6 once accumulated inventory is priced. The agreement to the
kilogram validates the instance, the technology matrix, the objective
and the evaluator against a figure published eighteen years earlier,
and it carries a managerial reading: on this order book the inventory
term costs nothing in service level, selecting among schedules of
equal shortage the one that holds least stock.

We deliberately refrain from attributing this reversal to solver
progress alone. Between the original experiments and ours, the solver
version, the machine, the number of threads, and, as Section~ef{sec:gamma} shows, the objective function itself all
changed; isolating any single factor would require the
machine-independent protocols of dedicated studies
\citep{koch2022progress,achterberg2013analyzing}, which are outside our
scope. The statement we do make is about practice rather than causes,
and it is the one that matters for the plant: what once justified
building heuristics is now dispatched by a general-purpose solver
within an operational deadline.

Two byproducts of our own campaign qualify how solver output should be
read at the larger scales. Incumbents are budget-nonmonotone: on three
instances the one-hour run returned a better solution than the
independent three-hour run, a consequence of different search
trajectories rather than of nondeterminism, since the parallel mode is
deterministic. And dual bounds are weak on a hard subclass, on
IncT5x\_10 the best known solution still lies about 17\% above the
three-hour bound, so true optimality gaps there are unknown, which is
why we report gaps against best-known solutions.

\subsection{What the excess penalty costs}
\label{sec:gamma}

The model of Section~\ref{sec:model} charges accumulated excess
production at $\gamma=0.001$ per unit and period; the original
formulation minimized shortage alone. Because everything else can be
held fixed, from instances, solver, machine, threads, tolerance, and
time limit, the two variants isolate the computational cost of that
single term. Table~\ref{tab:gamma} reports both on the 40
instances of sets 2X--5X at the three-hour budget.

\input{tables/tab_gamma}

The contrast is categorical (Table~\ref{tab:gamma}). Without the
holding term, the solver closes \emph{all forty} instances to proven
optimality, in mean times of 25, 48, 114, and 178~s for 2X, 3X, 4X,
and 5X respectively. With it, only the ten instances of 2X are closed
(mean 666~s); none of the thirty instances of 3X--5X is solved within
three hours, terminating with mean gaps of 1.7\%, 3.9\%, and 7.2\%.
A model that a laptop disposes of in under three minutes on the
longest set becomes, by the addition of a term weighted at one
thousandth, a model that three hours of the same solver on the same
machine leaves open. As required by construction,
$Z(\gamma=0)\le Z(\gamma=0.001)$ on every instance; the holding term
accounts for a median 13\% to 29\% of the objective, growing with the
horizon.

A second pattern in the same data explains why. Under $\gamma=0$ the
optimal value is \emph{invariant} to horizon replication: each base
order book yields the same total backlog whether it is repeated twice
or five times (mean 58{,}200.5 in all four sets). Backlog is incurred
only while production catches up at the beginning of the horizon; once
cumulative output overtakes cumulative demand it stays ahead, and
every additional block is free. Replication, therefore, adds periods
but no difficulty. Under $\gamma>0$ the same replication is not free:
the stock built up to avoid backlog is charged in every subsequent
period, the optimal value grows steadily with the horizon (63{,}780,
66{,}812, 69{,}842, and 73{,}403 on average for 2X--5X), and the
search must trade backlog against holding across the entire horizon
rather than resolving it once at the start. The scale axis of this
benchmark, in other words, is created by the holding term; we state
this explicitly because it delimits our results, which characterize
the difficulty of the backlog-plus-holding model rather than of
horizon length as such.

The mechanism is structural rather than numerical. Under
$\gamma=0$, once cumulative demand is covered any additional output is
free, so the feasible region contains large plateaus of equivalent
optimal schedules and the solver prunes aggressively. Under
$\gamma>0$, each of those schedules carries a different accumulated
excess, the plateaus collapse into a single optimum, and the linear
relaxation, which can spread production fractionally across
processes at negligible excess, becomes markedly weaker. The
practical lesson generalizes beyond this application: a secondary
objective term introduced for modeling realism, at a weight three
orders of magnitude below the primary criterion, can decide whether an
industrial lot-sizing model is routinely solvable or requires the
decomposition machinery of the following sections.

\subsection{Main comparison at the one-hour budget}
\label{sec:main3600}

Table~\ref{tab:main3600} reports, for all 60 instances at
3{,}600~s, the cold-started solver, \RFFO{}, \RFMIP{}, and the
\ILS{} baseline. Three findings organize the section. First, the
pure metaheuristic is dominated everywhere: \ILS{} best-of-ten trails
the solver's primal by an average margin that widens monotonically
with scale, from nil on the real order book, where it also reaches
the optimum, to 0.5\% on S, 2.6\% on 2X, and then 10.4\%, 16.4\%
and 23.9\% on 3X, 4X and 5X. It also trails
\RFFO{} on every large instance, despite its tenfold parallel
resources, evidence that, on this problem class, MIP-based
components are not optional refinements but the main engine.
Second, up to 5X ($|T|\le 95$) the monolithic solver remains the
method of choice at this budget: it wins the per-instance majority
in every set (10--0 on Real and 2X; 9--1 on 3X and 4X; 6--4 on 5X,
where \RFFO{} takes the four hardest instances and sets a new BKS on
IncT5x\_10, 4.6\% below the solver's three-hour incumbent). Third,
robustness: across seeds (grade~C), \RFFO{} shows zero variance on
five of twelve replicated instances and coefficients of variation
below 4\% elsewhere, with window-log audits confirming that
different seeds explore different window sequences
(Table~\ref{tab:seeds}).
Table~\ref{tab:stats} reports the paired Wilcoxon tests behind the
pooled comparisons.

\subsection{Operational budgets}
\label{sec:short}

At 600~s, the regime of daily re-planning, the picture is
scale-dependent (Table~\ref{tab:short600}). At the plant's own scale the question is moot:
the solver alone closes the instances in seconds, so the managerial
conclusion is direct and positive: order-book scheduling that was
heuristic territory in the original study is now re-optimized exactly
within any operational deadline. On the replicated scales
the decomposition methods become competitive from 3X upward (4--5 of
10 wins per set for \RF-based methods) and majority winners at 8X
(\RFMIP, 3/5) and 10X (\RFFO, 4/5). Aggregated over all 60 instances
the paired difference between \RFFO{} and the cold solver is not
significant (median $+0.12\%$, $p=0.27$), the small-instance cells
masking the large-scale advantage, which is precisely why we
report the frontier by cell rather than pooled. Against the
truncated \ILS{} trajectories, \RFFO{} wins all six large pilot
instances at 600~s with median advantage above 20\%
($p=0.031$). Hypothesis H1, whose decision rule required
decomposition to beat the cold solver in at least four of the six
pilot instances at both 300 and 600~s, was not supported (2/6 and
3/6): the original construction policy starved the improvement phase
at 300~s on $|T|=95$. A corrected policy devised after seeing that
outcome, which switches to greedy construction when the per-window
allowance falls below 10~s, repairs the failure cases and all four
regression cells then beat the \ILS{} reference; we report the
original outcome against the original criterion and treat the repair
as an exploratory follow-up.

\subsection{Scale-up and the method-choice frontier}
\label{sec:frontier}

The new 8X and 10X sets ($|T|=152$ and $190$; up to 30{,}210
binaries) locate the frontier. At 3{,}600~s the decomposition
methods win the majority in both sets: \RFMIP{} at 8X (3/5) and
\RFFO{} at 10X (3/5), with paired median advantage of 4.5\% for
\RFFO{} over the cold solver ($n=10$, $p=0.19$, rank-biserial
$-0.49$): consistent in direction, not statistically significant at
this sample size. Within the decomposition family the two variants
change places with scale: at 8X the warm-started monolith exploits
the solver's global view, while at 10X the model itself becomes the
bottleneck and window-based improvement scales better, on
IncT10x\_4, \RFMIP{} stalls at 376{,}198 while \RFFO{}, from the
same construction, reaches 172{,}983. Construction diagnostics
temper the story at the extreme scale: at $|T|\ge 152$ the windowed
relax-and-fix could not beat the greedy floor within its wall
budget, so all corrected runs start from the greedy solution and
the improvement phase does the heavy lifting; better construction
at extreme scale is an open problem we return to in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}.

The registered cross-budget question completes the map. Q5 asked
whether the cold solver, given 10{,}800~s, strictly beats the best
decomposition result at 3{,}600~s; the answer is \emph{yes} in both
sets (4/5 at 8X, 3/5 at 10X), improving six best-known solutions in
the process (Table~\ref{tab:q5}). Two 10X instances resist even the tripled budget
(IncT10x\_2 by 14.5\% and IncT10x\_6 by 2.1\%). The synthesis, Table~\ref{tab:frontier} and Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-heatmap}, is a method-choice map with
two genuine axes: at equal budgets, decomposition dominates from
$|T|\approx 150$ upward; granting the monolith one budget tier
($3\times$) restores its lead at 8/10 of the largest instances.
Decomposition, in other words, buys approximately one tier of solver
time, exactly the currency that matters in rolling re-planning.
We disclose the asymmetry that decompositions were not executed at
10{,}800~s; the released code and instances allow closing that cell.
Table~\ref{tab:scale-construction} reports the construction source and
remaining improvement time for the corrected scale-up runs.
